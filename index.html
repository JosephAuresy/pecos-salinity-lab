Scenarios

Scenario guide — what changes and why

Each scenario in the top-right dropdown snaps all sliders to a physically plausible configuration for the Pecos Basin. Click Load & go to lab on any card to apply it instantly, then adjust sliders one at a time to explore what drives each result.
Pristine Baseline

Pre-operation reference

The aquifer before any treated produced-water return. Low TDS loading, moderate natural recharge, relatively wet vadose zone, and no seasonal water-table swings. Use this as your “zero” to benchmark all other scenarios.

High TDS Loading

Elevated salinity scenario

Simulates a high-salinity discharge event: TDS at both the ag-field source and river return point are elevated, the vadose zone is drier than average (more capillary storage, slower drainage to the water table), and seasonal water-table swings periodically flush stored salt downward. Central question: does the salt plume reach Red Bluff Reservoir before diluting to safe levels?

Drought Year

Pecos Basin stress test

Recharge drops to near-zero, the river slows, and in-stream dilution weakens — typical of West Texas dry cycles. Tests whether salinity transport risk to Red Bluff Reservoir increases when natural flushing shuts down. Seasonal water-table swings remain active, periodically flushing stored salt downward with no dilution to offset it.

Pump & Treat

Active hydraulic control

Two extraction wells at high capacity (2 600 m³/d each) capture the salt plume before it reaches the river. Moderate TDS loading continues at the return point. Shows whether hydraulic containment can intercept the plume — and how pumping rate and well placement change the salt mass balance.

How to play — every control & guided experiments

Two contaminants leave the same places, but salt is conservative and PFAS sticks to air–water interfaces and soil. Change one thing at a time and watch the charts.

Clicks & buttons: ▸ click open ground on the map = add/remove a 🪣 well · ▸ click the river = place/move the 🛢 return point · ▸ 📡 Move monitor then click = relocate the receptor · ▸ click the cross-section = add/remove a well · ▸ 💥 / 💧 pulse buttons inject a one-time slug · ▸ Salt / PFAS source toggles · ▸ 🌊 Seasonal water table · ▸ Scenario presets (top right) snap everything to a setup.

Slider groups (accordions on the map panel): Sources · Treated return · Aquifer · PFAS chemistry, plus seasonal amplitude in the cross-section.

Guided experiments — do this → watch → conclude

1. The race. Hit 💥 Release pulse, Combined view. → Salt front sprints to the river while PFAS barely moves. Conclude: retardation alone separates two plumes from one source.

2. Air–water interface. Raise vadose deficit and Kawi. → mean R climbs, PFAS freezes near the field. Conclude: the unsaturated zone, not water chemistry, traps PFAS.

3. Soil vs air. Set Kawi=0, raise Kd only. → PFAS retards uniformly (no vadose concentration). Conclude: soil and air–water sorption are different fingerprints.

4. Cross-section trapping. 💧 Infiltrate pulse. → salt drains to the water table; PFAS pins in the vadose zone. Conclude: the legacy source lives above the water table.

5. Smear zone. Turn on 🌊 Seasonal water table, amplitude ~4 m. → PFAS spreads into a band at the moving fringe. Conclude: a fluctuating table builds a persistent smear zone.

6. Wells & rebound. Place wells between field and river. → salt drops at the well, PFAS keeps bleeding back. Conclude: pump-and-treat chases a vadose reservoir it can't pump.

7. Return-point siting. Move the 🛢 return: upstream of the reservoir vs. a gaining reach far below the wells. → compare river plume reach and river→aquifer leakage. Conclude: losing reaches and upstream-of-storage are worst.

8. Travel time. 📡 Move monitor onto the river, then an aquifer cell. → river breakthrough is fast/sharp; aquifer is delayed with a long PFAS tail. Conclude: two clocks — hours in the river, years in the aquifer.

9. Transformation. Lower the precursor half-life. → PFAS signal decays in time. Conclude: precursors convert and persist differently than a single tracer.

10. Dispersion & K. Raise D and K. Conclude: aquifer properties set the speed; retardation sets the difference between salt and PFAS.

TxPWC

Treated produced-water return — siting for safe beneficial use

Framed for the Texas Produced Water Consortium mission — finding where treated produced water can be returned with the least environmental risk. The goal is not "is discharge bad" but "where, and how treated, is a return point demonstrably safe?"
What you can analyze right here (toy model)

This sandbox lets you reason about the two return locations side by side:

• Agricultural field (land application) — the contaminant must cross the vadose zone. Salt drains to the water table; PFAS is held on air–water interfaces for years. The aquifer is hit first, the river only later.

• River return point (treated produced water) — moves downstream fast in the channel and, wherever the reach is losing, infiltrates into the aquifer and seeds a slow, retarded PFAS plume. On gaining reaches the river instead receives salty baseflow.

Move the 🛢 return point and watch the river plume reach, whether it backs toward Red Bluff Reservoir, whether it crosses your wells, and the river→aquifer leakage. Upstream of a gaining reach near the reservoir → worst case; a strongly gaining reach far below all wells → least harm.

What a coupled SWAT+ ＋ MODFLOW 6 + PFAS model would add

SWAT+Watershed surface & channel. Real time-varying streamflow per reach, so dilution of the return load is physically based — not a slider. Routes the load through Red Bluff Reservoir, giving concentration vs. time anywhere.

gwflow / MODFLOW 63-D subsurface. Multi-layer saturated–unsaturated flow, real river–aquifer exchange, heads & Darcy velocities in 3-D — plume direction, depth and travel time are computed, not assumed. Well capture zones are explicit.

PFAS module (USGT-PFAS)The chemistry. Air–water interface adsorption (Aawi=Amax(1−Sw), Langmuir), soil Kd, and precursor → terminal transformation. True retardation factors (often 10–100×) and per-species breakthrough.

Reach hydraulicsThe gap. Neither SWAT+ (1-D routing) nor MODFLOW 6 (river as a boundary) resolves where in the channel the plume goes — across to the far bank, over depth, and into the streambed. Fill it with HEC-RAS 2D (free, USACE, water-quality module, regulator-friendly) or Delft3D FM / EFDC for density-driven mixing (reservoir stratification). Toolchain: SWAT+ → HEC-RAS 2D / Delft3D FM → MODFLOW 6 + USGT-PFAS. The River-reach zoom in the Lab is a sketch of that step.

Siting questions, and how the model answers them

① Where will it go? In-stream routing maps affected downstream reaches; GWT + particle tracking maps the groundwater plume (down-gradient, toward gaining reaches and wells).

② How long will it take? Two clocks — the river (hours–days) and the aquifer (years–decades), PFAS slower by its retardation R. Output: breakthrough curves at each receptor.

③ Where does it harm the least? Rank candidate return points by — dilution capacity (high-flow reach → lower peak); reach type (gaining flushes out; losing creates a groundwater legacy); distance to receptors (downstream of wells, diversions, reservoir); storage risk (avoid upstream of Red Bluff).

④ Metrics to compare: peak & time-averaged concentration; exceedance of EPA 4 ng/L (PFOA/PFOS); dilution factor; mass split (river vs aquifer); time-to-threshold; plume footprint; fraction captured by wells; mass left on air–water interfaces after source removal.

⑤ Scenarios worth running: land application vs river return vs deep injection; different river kilometers; wet-year vs drought; with/without seasonal water-table swing; pump-and-treat given PFAS rebound.

Theory

From gwflow/SWAT+ to MODFLOW + USGT-PFAS — the equations

Why PFAS behaves so differently from salt, straight from the USG-Transport source code.
What changed

BEFOREgwflow (SWAT+) — per-cell water balance and salt as a conservative solute. Limitations: a single layer ⇒ only the plan view; no explicit unsaturated zone; solute not partitioned between water, soil and air.

NOWMODFLOW-USG / USGT-PFAS — unstructured grid, saturated–unsaturated flow (Richards), multi-species, and the decisive piece: air–water interface adsorption (Langmuir over Aawi=Amax(1−Sw)), soil Kd, and precursor transformation. The vertical cross-section is exactly what single-layer gwflow could not show.

The equations under the hood
gwflow water balance (explicit, per cell):
ht+1 = ht + Δt/(Sy·A)·[ ΣQlat + R·A − Qpump − Qgw–riv ]
Qlat = K·b̄·w·(hj−hi)/Δx  (Darcy between cells)
GW–river exchange (streambed conductance):
Qgw–riv = (Kbed/tbed)·L·W·(hgw − hriv)
Salt transport — conservative (R=1):
∂C/∂t = ∇·(D∇C) − v·∇C + source,   v = q/θ
PFAS transport — USGT-PFAS:
R·∂C/∂t = ∇·(D∇C) − v·∇C + source − λC
R = 1 + ρbKd/θ + Aawi·Kawi/θ  (retardation)
Aawi = Amax·(1 − Sw)  (air–water interface area)
Langmuir AWI isotherm:  s = Aawi·a·C/(1+b·C)

Source: gwt2aw_adsorb.f (AWI_AREA, AWI_KAWI, GWT2DCYAW/GWT2GENAW) in USG-Transport v2.7.0. Salt travels at v (R=1); PFAS at v/R, and R blows up in the unsaturated zone because (1−Sw) is large.

Which equations drive each view — Darcy, cone of depression, river routing

The lab solves the same physics a coupled gwflow / MODFLOW 6 model would, but in a simplified explicit 2-D scheme. Here is exactly what governs each panel, and which classic equations are — and are not — used.

▲ Plan view (map) — groundwater flow

USED Darcy's law + mass balance → 2-D transient unconfined (Boussinesq) flow, solved explicitly each step:

Sy·∂h/∂t = ∂/∂x(K·b·∂h/∂x) + ∂/∂y(K·b·∂h/∂y) + N − Qwell/A − Qriv/A

Discrete form in code:
ht+1 = ht + Δt/(SyA)·[ Σnb K·b·Δx·(hnb−h)/Δx + N·A − Qriv − ΣQwell ]
Inter-cell flux is Darcy: Q = K·b·w·∂h/∂x. Solute is carried at the seepage velocity
v = −(K/θ)·∇h  (Darcy flux divided by porosity).
Sliders: K (hydraulic conductivity), Sy (specific yield), R/N (recharge).

▲ Plan view — pumping wells (cone of depression)

Each well is a point sink that removes Qw from its cell:
hwell ← hwell − Δt·Qw/(SyA)

The cone of depression is an emergent result of re-solving the head field around the sink each step — the surrounding cells draw down as water flows toward the well.
NOT USED We do not impose an analytical drawdown. For reference, the classic closed-form cones are
Theis (transient): s = Q/(4πT)·W(u),  u = r²S/(4Tt)
Thiem (steady): s = Q/(2πT)·ln(R/r)
— valid only for a homogeneous infinite aquifer. The numerical sink reproduces the same cone shape while honouring the river boundary and heterogeneity.
Slider: Qw (pumping rate). Click open ground on the map to add/remove a well.

▲ Plan view — river ↔ aquifer exchange

USED Streambed conductance (a head-dependent / Cauchy boundary, identical to the MODFLOW RIV package):

Qgw–riv = (Kbed/tbed)·L·W·(hgw − hriv)
hgw < hriv → losing reach (river water + any residual salt/PFAS infiltrate the aquifer).
hgw > hriv → gaining reach (groundwater and its salt discharge to the river).
Slider: Kbed (streambed conductivity).

▲ In-stream routing — how the river plume moves

USED 1-D advective (plug-flow) tracer routing with first-order in-stream attenuation, solved upwind and CFL-substepped:

∂C/∂t = −V·∂C/∂x − kdil·C
NOT USED No Saint-Venant (no momentum equation, no stage–discharge), no Muskingum hydrologic routing. The stream velocity V is prescribed (slider), the stage is fixed, and only the solute is routed — bed exchange with the aquifer uses the streambed-conductance term above. A full study would route flow with HEC-RAS 2D / Saint-Venant first, then transport.
Sliders: Vriv (stream velocity), kdil (in-stream dilution/decay), return salt / PFAS / NH₃ loads.

▼ Lateral view (cross-section) — vadose + saturated

USED Two-zone velocity field, then the advection–dispersion equation with retardation in (x, z):

Vadose (z < zwt): downward infiltration vz = vinf(R),   vx ≈ 0
Saturated (z ≥ zwt): lateral flow vx = vlat(K),   vz ≈ 0

R·∂C/∂t = Dx∂²C/∂x² + Dz∂²C/∂z² − vx∂C/∂x − vz∂C/∂z − λC
R = 1 + Kd + Kawi·(1−Sw)/θ, with water saturation Sw rising with depth to 1 at the water table. The water table zwt(x) slopes DOWN toward the river (gaining river): head decreases toward the Pecos, so groundwater and its dissolved load discharge there. Seasonal fluctuation zwt ± A·sin(2πt/365) builds the PFAS smear zone; well pumping adds a Gaussian drawdown cone.
Sliders: Vadose deficit (1−Sw), Kawi, Kd, recharge R, seasonal amplitude, Qw.

Scheme: explicit finite differences (forward Euler in time, central dispersion, first-order upwind advection), CFL-limited time step. This favours clarity and interactivity over the implicit, mass-conservative solvers (MODFLOW 6, MT3D-USGS, USG-Transport) used for quantitative work.

⚠️ This is a conceptual/educational 2-D model — it reproduces the behaviour of the USGT-PFAS equations (air–water retardation, vadose-zone trapping), not a full MODFLOW-USG run on an unstructured grid.

Grid resolution & numerical dispersion — why cell size matters

The Lab tab has an interactive toggle (Grid Δx buttons in the plan-view toolbar) where you can switch the 2-D simulation between Δx = 500 m, 100 m, and 50 m and watch the salt front smear or sharpen in real time. The diagrams below show the quantitative basis: the same 1-D pulse, same physics (v = 1.5 m/d, D = 5 m²/d), solved at T = 300 d with a first-order upwind finite-difference scheme.

Δx = 500 m — Coarse
Dnum = 225 m²/d (45× physical D). Salt arrives weeks early; front is completely smeared. A model built on this grid would report safe TDS levels long before the plume actually disperses.
Δx = 100 m — Standard
Dnum = 45 m²/d (9× physical D). Front is sharper but still noticeably smeared near the 500 mg/L drinking-water threshold. Typical of older MODFLOW structured grids (DIS).
Δx = 25 m — Fine ✔ MODFLOW 6
Dnum = 11.25 m²/d (2.25× physical D). Front closely tracks the analytical solution. MODFLOW 6 DISV achieves this only in critical zones.
Numerical diffusion formula (upwind FD):  Dnum = v·Δx·(1−Cr)/2 — where Cr = v·Δt/Δx is the Courant number. Coarser cells → larger Dnum → artificially smeared salt front.
MODFLOW 6 DISV solution for the Pecos Basin:  DISV 25 m cells along the river channel and return-point zone — DIS 200–500 m cells for the background aquifer — GWT (or MT3D-USGS) solves transport on the same flexible grid. Total cell count < 30 k; run time < 2 min on a laptop.

Limitations & full model

Beyond this conceptual model — the full Pecos Basin analysis

This lab is a teaching simulation — it captures the physics of PFAS vs. salt transport in a simplified 2-D domain but leaves out most of what drives contaminant fate in a real catchment. Understanding what is missing is just as important as understanding what is here.

What this model does NOT include:

  • No real meteorology — no spatially distributed rainfall, no actual evapotranspiration, no snowmelt, no seasonal variability in recharge driven by climate
  • No land surface processes — no land use map, no soil profiles (texture, organic matter, hydraulic properties), no terrain slopes, no surface runoff, no erosion or sediment transport
  • No HRUs or subbasins — the 309 subbasins and ∼100+ Hydrologic Response Units (HRUs) in the SWAT+ model are collapsed to a single 2-D slab
  • No agricultural water use — no irrigation scheduling, no crop demand, no return-flow from irrigated fields back to the aquifer
  • No pumping inventory — only 2 conceptual wells; the real basin has hundreds of irrigation and municipal wells each removing water and changing heads
  • No 3-D flow — a single unconfined layer; no vertical head gradients, no multi-aquifer interaction, no confining layers
  • No reservoir operations — Red Bluff Reservoir release rules, evaporation, and stratification are not simulated
  • No extreme events — drought multi-year sequences and flood pulses that drive non-linear transport are represented only by slider changes, not by real time series

gwflowThe shallow alluvial aquifer (already calibrated for this basin, p_29hru_t10m). Receives percolation from each SWAT+ HRU, exchanges water with the river via streambed conductance (Kbed=0.005 m/d from the gwflow.ini you can inspect with the ℹ buttons above), and routes salt as a conservative solute. Single-layer, explicit solver.

MODFLOW 6The step up. Replace the single gwflow layer with a 3-D unstructured grid (DISV or DISU packages). Cells are small near the river, wells, and reservoir; large in the interior. Multi-layer captures vertical gradients in head and concentration — critical where PFAS sorbs differently at different depths. The Newton-Raphson formulation handles drying and rewetting during drought cycles.

USGT-PFASThe chemistry layer runs inside MT3D-USGS on the MODFLOW 6 heads. Adds air-water interface adsorption (this simulation’s “R” factor), soil Kd, and precursor transformation — so you get per-compound, per-depth breakthrough curves at every receptor.

Surface hydrology — what land use, soils, and slopes actually control

Land use Where water goes first

  • Irrigated cropland — deep percolation below field capacity delivers water (and dissolved contaminants) directly to the water table; surface residue slows evaporation and infiltration
  • Rangeland/shrub — low interception, high bare-soil evaporation, minimal deep percolation except in rock fractures
  • Riparian vegetation — high ET draws groundwater to the surface; acts as a bio-filter but also concentrates dissolved contaminants
  • Impervious areas (roads, pads, tanks) — concentrate runoff, short-circuit infiltration, generate first-flush pulses of contaminants

Soil profiles Storage and delay

  • Caliche layers (common in the Permian Basin) — act as perching layers, creating ephemeral water tables and storing PFAS above the regional aquifer
  • Organic matter — Kd for PFAS scales with soil organic carbon; sandy alluvial soils along the Pecos have low OC — less soil sorption but faster transit
  • Clay lenses — slow vertical movement, promote lateral spreading; a contaminant can travel kilometers horizontally before reaching the water table
  • Texture gradient — coarse to fine transitions create capillary barriers that redirect vadose-zone flow laterally

Slopes & HRUs Routing the runoff

  • Steep slopes along canyon walls — rapid overland flow, minimal infiltration, delivers contaminants to channels in hours not years
  • Flat valley floor — water ponds, long contact time, dominates recharge; the risk zone for vadose-zone PFAS accumulation
  • HRU connectivity — SWAT+ routes lateral flow and return flow between HRUs within a subbasin; a contaminated upslope HRU can drain into a downslope well-field HRU even if they are not connected by a channel

ET & climate The invisible sink

  • Potential ET in the Trans-Pecos averages 2 200–2 500 mm/yr vs. 250–400 mm/yr of precipitation — actual recharge is the small residual of a large difference; errors in ET propagate directly to errors in plume dilution
  • Plant-uptake root zone — crops remove water (and some contaminants) from the top 0.5–1.5 m; below the root zone, movement is advection-dominated with no further biological processing
  • Irrigation return flow — roughly 20–40% of applied irrigation water percolates below the root zone; in the lower Pecos Valley this is a major recharge source and a salinity vector
Agricultural water use — crops, irrigation, and what that means for contaminant transport

The Pecos Valley is one of the most intensively irrigated semi-arid valleys in Texas. Key crops and their implications:

Cotton High-water, shallow-root

  • Dominates Reeves, Ward, and Winkler counties — roughly 60–80 000 irrigated acres along the Pecos Valley
  • Flood-irrigated fields generate large percolation pulses; furrow irrigation distributes water unevenly, creating high-recharge strips aligned with row direction
  • Pesticide and nutrient co-application means PFAS plumes may co-migrate with nitrate and herbicides — important for risk stacking

Alfalfa Year-round heavy user

  • 6–8 cuttings per year; irrigation during winter months when ET demand is low — maximizes deep percolation and aquifer recharge from fields
  • Deep roots (1.5–2 m) can draw up shallow groundwater, short-circuiting the vadose zone and potentially mobilizing PFAS stored at the capillary fringe
  • Saline irrigation water (Pecos River TDS 1 500–5 000 mg/L depending on reach) accumulates salts in the root zone over decades

Pecans & onions Specialty crops

  • Pecans near Carlsbad NM and Fort Stockton TX use drip or micro-sprinkler irrigation — lower deep-percolation rates but concentrated at specific locations along the river corridor
  • Onion fields in the Presidio valley (lower basin) use intensive sprinkler irrigation in short windows — high recharge rates concentrated in March–May
  • Specialty crop areas often coincide with the best-quality alluvial groundwater — the most sensitive zones for PFAS contamination

Irrigation scheduling The model doesn’t know when

  • SWAT+ uses a crop calendar and auto-irrigation trigger (soil water threshold) to schedule applications — this drives the temporal pattern of recharge and hence plume velocity
  • A dry June followed by heavy July irrigation creates a recharge pulse that pushes a PFAS slug downward much faster than the annual-average slider implies
  • Deficit irrigation (common during drought) reduces deep percolation but concentrates salts — another trade-off that only a time-stepping model captures
All the wells — the pumping inventory MODFLOW 6 would track

This simulation has two conceptual capture wells. The real Pecos Basin has hundreds of active wells that collectively reshape the water table and every transport pathway. A full MODFLOW 6 model would include:

Irrigation wells

  • Alluvial aquifer irrigation wells throughout Reeves, Ward, and Winkler counties — typical rates 500–3 000 m³/d each, seasonal (April–September)
  • Water-level declines of 1–5 m over decades in some areas reverse natural baseflow direction — river reaches that were once gaining become losing, accelerating contaminant transfer from river to aquifer
  • Cone of depression from a cluster of wells can capture a PFAS plume that would otherwise reach the river — or draw it toward a domestic supply

Municipal wells

  • Pecos TX, Fort Stockton TX, Carlsbad NM, and smaller communities depend on alluvial and deeper aquifer wells for drinking water
  • These are the highest-priority receptors; MODFLOW 6 capture-zone analysis would delineate the time-of-travel zones that define PFAS risk to each well
  • Red Bluff Reservoir water (which receives PFAS-laden baseflow if the aquifer is contaminated) supplements municipal supply via the RBWUA

Oil & gas water wells

  • Produced-water disposal wells (Class II UIC) in the Permian Basin create downward hydraulic gradients — adjacent freshwater aquifers may see induced upward leakage from deeper saline formations if casing integrity fails
  • Water-source wells for hydraulic fracturing operations tap shallow alluvial or deeper Dockum aquifer — can lower heads enough to reverse gradients locally

Livestock & stock tanks

  • Small stock wells (5–50 m³/d) are numerous throughout the basin — individually minor but collectively significant in areas of intensive ranching
  • Stock tanks (earthen impoundments) create local recharge cells and can concentrate PFAS from surface runoff — a direct exposure pathway for cattle and wildlife
Drought and floods — how extremes flip the transport picture

The Pecos Basin is hydrologically extreme. Single events or multi-year sequences can completely change the direction and rate of contaminant transport. A slider for “recharge = 0.05” approximates a drought but cannot capture the non-linear dynamics:

Drought What changes

  • Water-table decline — the capillary fringe drops, increasing the vadose zone thickness; PFAS stored at the former fringe is left dry and adsorbed to air-water interfaces at that elevation — it won’t move again until recharge returns
  • Losing reaches expand — as river stage drops, more reaches switch from gaining to losing; the river becomes a source of contaminant to the aquifer rather than a sink
  • Well interference — with less recharge to recover pumped volumes, cones of depression from irrigation wells may merge, creating regional capture of contaminant plumes toward agricultural supply wells
  • Reservoir concentrating effect — Red Bluff Reservoir loses volume through evaporation (high in West Texas); residual TDS and PFAS concentrations can double or triple during multi-year droughts even with no new loading
  • La Niña cycles (dominant in the Pecos) can produce 2–4 consecutive dry years; the 2011 drought was the most severe in recorded Pecos River history

Floods What changes

  • Rapid recharge pulse — a single large event can add more water to the aquifer in days than years of normal recharge; this piston-flow effect pushes a PFAS slug that had been held in the vadose zone down to the water table almost instantaneously
  • Losing-to-gaining reversal — during flood stage the river head rises above groundwater; every reach becomes strongly losing, delivering a concentrated pulse of TDS and surface-accumulated PFAS into the aquifer along the entire river corridor
  • Inundation of fields and well pads — surface flooding mobilizes stored PFAS from soil surfaces and potentially short-circuits wellhead protection areas
  • Streambed flushing — high velocity scours streambed sediments that have sequestered PFAS; releases a secondary pulse into the water column downstream (including into Red Bluff Reservoir)
  • El Niño years typically bring above-normal precipitation to the Pecos; the contrast between El Niño and La Niña cycles drives multi-decade variability in aquifer storage and transport rates
MODFLOW 6 unstructured grid — where fine resolution matters most

The unstructured discretization (DISV/DISU) in MODFLOW 6 is not just a computational convenience — it is the key to answering the question “where exactly does the plume go?” rather than “which direction?”

Grid design Fine where it matters

  • Near wells — cells refined to 10–50 m to resolve cone of depression and capture zone; coarser (500 m–1 km) in the interior where head gradients are flat
  • Along the river and reservoir — strip refinement follows the channel geometry (using the SWAT+ rivs1.shp network already in this lab); accurately resolves bank-storage exchange and the gaining/losing transition
  • At sensitive receptors — municipal wells, stock ponds, springs — refined cells so breakthrough curves are computed at the actual point, not averaged over a large cell
  • DISV vs DISU — DISV (structured by layer, unstructured in plan) is simpler to set up; DISU (fully unstructured in 3D) allows locally refined vertical layers near the water table or a perching layer

Analyses enabled

  • Plume footprint — 50, 100, and 500 µg/L PFAS iso-concentration surfaces in 3-D, updated each stress period (monthly)
  • Capture-zone mapping — backward particle tracking (MODPATH 7) from each receptor well defines which land area contributes recharge to that well within 5, 10, 25, and 50 years
  • Sensitivity and uncertainty — PEST++ or PEST ensemble smoother samples the parameter space (K, Sy, Kbed, dispersivity, Kd, Kawi) and produces probabilistic plume envelopes rather than single best-estimate
  • Optimization — MODFLOW 6 + PEST can minimize pumping cost subject to concentration constraints at receptors — i.e., find the cheapest pump-and-treat design that keeps PFAS below MCL at all wells
Sensitive areas in the Pecos Basin — people, livestock, ecosystems, and water supply

Not all areas in the basin carry equal risk. A full analysis would identify and rank sensitive receptors and design the monitoring network and protection zones around them:

🚰 People & drinking water

  • Red Bluff Reservoir — the primary water supply for the Red Bluff Water & Power Control District (RBWPCD), serving parts of Loving, Winkler, and Ward counties; PFAS in reservoir = direct human exposure via tap water
  • City of Pecos, Fort Stockton, Carlsbad NM — municipal supply wells in the alluvial aquifer; time-of-travel analysis needed to define PFAS vulnerability
  • Rural domestic wells — approximately 2 000–5 000 private wells in the basin; no centralized monitoring; highest uncertainty population exposure

🌾 Agriculture & livestock

  • Irrigated crops — if irrigation water is drawn from a contaminated aquifer, PFAS uptake into cotton, alfalfa, and vegetable crops is a documented concern; bioaccumulation in the food chain
  • Livestock watering — cattle and sheep water from stock wells and surface water throughout the basin; PFAS accumulates in tissue; relevant for beef and dairy production
  • Stock tanks — earthen impoundments collect runoff — a concentrated PFAS exposure point for livestock during drought when no other surface water is available

🐌 Ecosystems & wildlife

  • Pecos pupfish (Cyprinodon pecosensis) — federal species of concern; endemic to the Pecos River; highly sensitive to salinity changes and potentially to PFAS bioaccumulation in invertebrate food chain
  • Pecos sunflower (Helianthus paradoxus) — federally threatened; grows in cienegas (spring-fed wetlands) that are fed by shallow groundwater; vadose-zone PFAS that reaches the water table reaches these habitats
  • Riparian corridor — cottonwood-willow galleries along the Pecos draw groundwater to the surface via ET; concentrate dissolved contaminants in root zone and biomass
  • Migratory bird stopover — Red Bluff Reservoir is an important stopover on the Central Flyway; waterfowl exposure through fish and invertebrate consumption

💧 Water infrastructure

  • Red Bluff Dam & Reservoir — if PFAS enters the reservoir, it concentrates during low-water periods; drawdown exposes contaminated sediments and re-releases PFAS; treatment costs rise sharply
  • RBWPCD delivery canals — open-channel delivery from the reservoir to farm headgates; a secondary evaporation and concentration step
  • Trans-Pecos Water Authority infrastructure — inter-basin transfer proposals (e.g., Pecos to Midland-Odessa) could spread contamination geographically if source water is not screened
  • Rio Grande below confluence — the Pecos River joins the Rio Grande near Amistad Reservoir; contamination crossing the state line or international border triggers federal and bi-national reporting obligations
What a fully coupled model would unlock — the analysis portfolio

Combining SWAT+ (calibrated) + MODFLOW 6 (unstructured, multi-layer) + USGT-PFAS + PEST++ opens a portfolio of analyses that are impossible with this conceptual tool:

01

Plume arrival time at Red Bluff Reservoir — for each return location and treatment quality, probabilistic breakthrough curves at the reservoir intake with uncertainty bands

02

Agricultural water-use impact — which irrigation wells draw from the contaminated plume, and in which year; crop PFAS uptake potential per parcel

03

Drought scenario stress test — simulate a 2011-type multi-year drought on top of a produced-water return; show how reservoir concentration triples while remediation becomes ineffective

04

Flood-driven remobilization — quantify the PFAS mass released from the vadose zone by a 25-year flood event and its travel time to the reservoir

05

Optimal pump-and-treat design — minimum number, location, and pumping rate of extraction wells to keep PFAS below the EPA MCL (4 ng/L) at all municipal receptors

06

Return location ranking — rank all potential treated produced-water return sites in the basin by PFAS arrival time at the nearest sensitive receptor, with and without treatment to below MCL

07

Sensitive-area protection zones — for each receptor (reservoir, municipal wells, pupfish habitat), delineate the land area that must be managed to prevent PFAS arrival within 10, 25, and 50 years

08

Reservoir operations optimization — couple the reservoir water-quality model with Red Bluff release rules to determine whether adjusted release timing can dilute PFAS plumes arriving at the dam

TxPWC mission Every one of these analyses frames treated produced-water beneficial use as a siting and treatment quality decision — not as a binary “safe or not” question. The goal is to provide regulators, operators, and communities with the spatial and temporal evidence needed to answer “where and how treated is it demonstrably safe?” with quantitative uncertainty bounds.
